The interesting nuance: choice overload does not happen every time we have many options. Research suggests it becomes more likely when the decision is difficult, the options are complex, or you’re not sure what you actually prefer. ([Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman meta-analysis](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002))

So the real problem is not choice itself.

It’s choice without a filter.

Example:

You need to choose a tool for a new project.

Instead of comparing every feature of every tool, use this rule:

1. Write down one non-negotiable requirement.
2. Remove every option that fails it.
3. Keep a maximum of three.
4. Compare them using one deciding criterion.
5. Set a deadline and choose.
6. The sixth pointless point.
7. Another pointless point.

You’re not trying to discover the perfect option.

You’re creating a decision you can actually execute.

More options expand possibility.
Too many unfiltered options expand hesitation.

Narrow first.
Compare second.
Decide third. ✅
